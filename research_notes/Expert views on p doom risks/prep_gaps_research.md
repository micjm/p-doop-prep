## Q1: Potassium iodide (KI) guidance — who, when, buy?

### Takeaway
US public-health guidance (CDC) is consistent and cautious: KI protects only the thyroid, only against radioactive iodine, and should only be taken when public-health/emergency officials advise it — it is not a general "nuclear pill." Ready.gov's main radiation page does not mention KI at all, yet FDA-approved KI (e.g., ThyroSafe) is freely sold OTC in the US (Amazon/Walmart/eBay), so household-buy guidance is genuinely unfilled on most prep sites.

### Cited Findings
- CDC (FAQ page, Wayback capture Apr 23, 2026): "People should only take KI (potassium iodide) on the advice of public health or emergency management officials. There are health risks associated with taking KI." — [CDC Radiation Emergencies FAQ (archived 2026-04-23)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260423025454/https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/faq/)
- CDC infographic "How Potassium Iodide (KI) Works" (page dated Apr 17, 2024): KI fills the thyroid with stable iodine for ~24 hours so it rejects radioactive iodine; it does not block other radionuclides or reverse damage already done; do not substitute table salt, iodine-rich food, or supplements; only use FDA-approved KI products; "Only take KI on the advice of a medical doctor, public health, or emergency management officials"; overdosing has serious health risks — [CDC KI infographic (archived 2024-06-06)](https://web.archive.org/web/20240606003458/https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/infographic/potassium-iodide.html) (live URL: https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/infographic/potassium-iodide.html)
- Ready.gov "Radiation Emergencies" (page footer: Last Updated 01/07/2025) gives zero KI guidance; its protective-action advice is entirely "Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned," shelter-in-place 24h, decontamination (removing outer clothing removes up to 90% of radioactive material) — [Ready.gov Radiation Emergencies](https://www.ready.gov/radiation)
- FDA guidance documents exist but consumer-facing KI URL appears to have moved (old URLs 404 as of Oct 2026): "(Potassium Iodide) KI in Radiation Emergencies — Questions and Answers" and "Potassium Iodide as a Thyroid Blocking Agent in Radiation Emergencies" — [FDA KI Q&A guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/potassium-iodide-ki-radiation-emergencies-questions-and-answers); [FDA thyroid blocking agent guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/potassium-iodide-thyroid-blocking-agent-radiation-emergencies) — **located via search, not fetched (flag: verify current content)**
- Other agency leads (located, not fetched): NRC "Frequently Asked Questions About Potassium Iodide" — [NRC KI FAQ](https://www.nrc.gov/about-nrc/emerg-preparedness/about-emerg-preparedness/potassium-iodide/ki-faq); HHS REMM "Potassium Iodide (KI)" — [REMM KI](https://remm.hhs.gov/potassiumiodide.htm) — **flag as secondhand-unverified**
- Product availability (US, current): ThyroSafe (SERB Pharmaceuticals; site footer "© 2026 … US-THY-2600026 / April 2026") markets FDA-approved 65 mg and 130 mg KI tablets "for use when public officials give the word," with a Where-to-Buy page linking Amazon, Walmart, and eBay — [ThyroSafe](https://thyrosafe.com/); [ThyroSafe where to buy](https://thyrosafe.com/where-to-buy)
- Retail listings confirm consumer availability: Amazon and Walmart listings for ThyroSafe 65 mg/130 mg packs surfaced in search — [Walmart ThyroSafe 65mg 40ct](https://www.walmart.com/ip/15745408550) (**flag: third-party retail, prices not captured**)
- Inference support: ThyroSafe marketing explicitly positions OTC purchase as household preparedness while deferring ingestion to officials — consistent with CDC "only on advice of officials" framing.

### Inferences
- The practical, defensible household-prep position is: yes, FDA-approved KI can be bought OTC and stored (esp. near nuclear power plants — ThyroSafe itself markets a "reactor locator" citing 90+ US reactors), but prep guidance must pair purchase with strict "do not take until officials advise" language and thyroid-only/limited-duration caveats.
- Ready.gov's omission of KI is a real content gap a prep site could fill by citing CDC rather than improvising.

### Gaps
- Current FDA consumer-education page content could not be fetched (URLs moved); dosing-by-age tables not captured — do not state doses without fetching FDA/CDC dosing tables first.
- No data captured on state/local KI distribution programs (e.g., state stockpiles near reactors) or on UK/WHO/IAEA guidance for comparison — not researched.
- Price points for KI packs not captured (retail pages not opened).

## Q2: Distance from nuclear targets — least-exposed regions + official shelter/evacuation guidance

### Takeaway
Official US guidance (Ready.gov/FEMA) deliberately avoids "where is safest" and instead gives a universal rule: after a detonation you have "10 minutes or more" to reach good shelter (basement/center of brick or concrete building), stay 24 hours, HVAC off. Open-source target/fallout analysis exists (FAS Nuclear Targeting Project, NUKEMAP, USA Today fallout maps), but I found no rigorous, current, peer-reviewed ranking of "safest US regions" — most "safest states" pieces are secondhand map content farms or news aggregators.

### Cited Findings
- Ready.gov (Last Updated 01/07/2025): "After a detonation, you will have 10 minutes or more to find an adequate shelter before fallout arrives"; reach a multi-story building or basement within a few minutes; safest buildings have brick or concrete walls; underground parking/subways good; remain in basement/center of large building for first 24 hours unless immediate hazard or authorities say leave; radiation "decreases rapidly… during the first 24 hours"; shut off fans/AC/forced-air, close windows/doors/dampers; sheltering usually ≥24h; evacuate only when instructed — [Ready.gov Radiation Emergencies](https://www.ready.gov/radiation) (identical text in [2024 Wayback capture](https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://www.ready.gov/nuclear-explosion))
- Ready.gov blast advice: if warned, take cover behind anything possible; lie face down outdoors to protect from heat/debris; after shockwave, go inside nearest building ASAP — same page
- Ready.gov links FEMA PrepTalk by Brooke Buddemeier, "Saving Lives After a Nuclear Detonation" (recommends deep shelter in basements/interior rooms of substantial buildings) — [FEMA PrepTalks](https://www.fema.gov/blog/preptalks-brooke-buddemeier-saving-lives-after-nuclear-detonation) — **linked from Ready.gov; not independently fetched**
- FAS Nuclear Targeting Project (page dated 04.29.26): NRDC+FAS open-source project estimating "types and number of targets the United States may hold at risk in Russia and China," arsenal sizing implications, and "radioactive fallout effects of nuclear employment if predicted war plans were executed"; builds on a 2001 NRDC study; notes New START expiration Feb 2026 — [FAS Nuclear Targeting Project](https://fas.org/initiative/nuclear-targeting-project/)
- FAS June 2025 report "Planning for the Unthinkable: The targeting strategies of…" — [FAS PDF](https://fas.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/June_2025_Planning-for-the-Unthinkable.pdf) — **located via search, not fetched (flag)**
- NUKEMAP (Alex Wellerstein) project pages — [alexwellerstein.com/projects/nukemap](https://alexwellerstein.com/projects/nukemap/); [nuclearsecrecy.com/nukemap](https://nuclearsecrecy.com/nukemap/) — **located; nukemap.org/blog endpoints unreachable during research (DNS/403); no specific Wellerstein "safest region" essay verified**
- US nuclear forces reference: FAS NukeGuide USA — [nuke.fas.org/guide/usa](https://nuke.fas.org/guide/usa/) (**located, not fetched**)
- Counterforce-relevant secondhand analysis: USA Today interactive "Fallout maps show how an attack on nuclear silos would impact US cities" — [USA Today fallout map](https://www.usatoday.com/graphics/interactives/us-nuclear-weapons-expansion-fallout-map/) — **secondhand, not fetched**
- "Safest states" media wave (Mar 2026), all bot-blocked or aggregator-grade — flag as low-quality: [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/safest-us-states-nuclear-war-attack-us-silos-1995408), [al.com](https://www.al.com/news/2026/03/chilling-map-shows-4-safest-us-states-in-the-event-of-a-nuclear-war.html), [cleveland.com](https://www.cleveland.com/news/2026/03/new-map-suggests-where-americans-might-survive-nuclear-war.html)
- FEMA planning documents located (not fetched; fema.gov 403): [FEMA nuclear detonation planning guide PDF](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/documents/fema_nuc-detonation-planning-guide.pdf); [72-Hour Nuclear Response Guidance](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/documents/fema_oet-72-hour-nuclear-detonation-response-guidance.pdf); [2010 Planning Guidance (archive.org)](https://archive.org/details/fema_planning-guidance-response-nuclear-detonation); HHS REMM mirror [PlanningGuidanceNuclearDetonation.pdf](https://remm.hhs.gov/PlanningGuidanceNuclearDetonation.pdf) — **flag: verify before citing specifics beyond Ready.gov's 10-minute/24-hour rules**

### Inferences
- A prep site can responsibly extend Ready.gov by: (a) quoting the 10-min/24-h shelter rules verbatim, (b) noting official doctrine ignores relocation geography in favor of immediate shelter, and (c) pointing power users at FAS/NUKEMAP for target/fallout exploration while explicitly labeling "safest state" listicles as unvetted.
- The March 2026 "safest states" viral maps appear to be based on fallout-from-silo-counterforce scenarios (per headlines referencing silos); underlying methodology not verifiable from fetched sources.

### Gaps
- No verified published counterforce target list or "least-exposed region" academic analysis was captured; FAS targeting outputs are Russia/China-focused per the project page, not a US-safest-zones ranking.
- NUKEMAP itself and Wellerstein's blog were unreachable (blog.nukemap.org DNS failure; wellerstein.net Cloudflare 403) — any specific NUKEMAP-based regional claim remains unverified.
- FEMA nuclear planning PDFs not opened (403); evacuation-route detail beyond "listen to authorities" not captured.
- Wind-drift/fallout-pattern guidance (e.g., upwind vs. downwind siting) not found in fetched official pages.

## Q3: Household communications when networks fail — ham/GMRS/FRS/satellite

### Takeaway
Licensing costs are modest and now uniform ($35 FCC application fee for amateur and GMRS personal licenses, 10-year term); GMRS needs a license but no exam, FRS needs no license at all, ham (Technician) requires passing a 35-question exam. Satellite messengers (Garmin inReach) exist as the no-infrastructure option, but current subscription pricing could not be scraped from Garmin's JS-gated pages — treat dollar figures as a gap pending a manual check.

### Cited Findings
- Ham licensing structure (ARRL, fetched Oct 2026): three US classes — Technician (entry level; one 35-question exam on theory/regulations/operating practice; privileges above 30 MHz plus limited HF), General (additional 35-question exam; worldwide HF), Extra (50-question exam; full privileges); US licenses valid 10 years; anyone may hold one except representatives of foreign governments; in-person and online exams regularly available — [ARRL Getting Licensed](https://www.arrl.org/getting-licensed)
- FCC application fees (Schedule of Application Fees, Federal Register final rule, pub. 2025-04-23): personal licenses (explicitly including Amateur Radio Service and GMRS) — "New license, modification … $35", "Renewal … $35", "Vanity Call Sign (Amateur Radio Service) … $35" — [FR 2025-06899](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/23/2025-06899/schedule-of-application-fees) (table text confirmed via Federal Register full-text endpoint)
- GMRS rules (eCFR 47 CFR 95 Subpart E, current as fetched Oct 2026): "A valid individual license is required to operate a GMRS station"; individual applicants must be ≥18 (§95.1705(a)(1)); licensee may allow immediate family members to operate, and "any individual … if necessary to communicate an emergency message" (§95.1705(c)); license term "normally … ten years" (§95.1705(e)); service is "short-distance two-way voice communication … including … emergencies and natural disasters" (§95.1703) — [eCFR Part 95 Subpart E](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter/D/part-95/subpart-E) (content retrieved via eCFR renderer API)
- GMRS vs. exam difficulty: GMRS requires payment of fee but no examination element in the rules (eligibility per §95.305 health/no-restriction-type conditions; contrast with amateur exams above) — inference from eCFR text; **no FCC "how hard" statement exists (rules are procedural only)**
- FRS (eCFR 47 CFR 95 Subpart B): "short-distance two-way voice communication service, with limited data applications, between low power hand-held radios"; FRS units may interoperate with GMRS stations and may exchange location/brief text data (§95.531); no individual license requirement appears in Subpart B (unlike GMRS §95.1705) — [eCFR Part 95 Subpart B](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter/D/part-95/subpart-B) (retrieved via eCFR renderer API) — **inference: license-free by structure; flag for confirmation against §95.501-era reserved sections**
- Ready.gov recommends "a battery-powered or hand crank radio, which will continue to work if other forms of communication fail" plus FEMA App/community alerts and an out-of-state contact in the family communications plan — [Ready.gov Radiation Emergencies](https://www.ready.gov/radiation); [Ready.gov Make a Plan](https://www.ready.gov/make-a-plan)
- Satellite messengers: Garmin maintains an official "inReach Consumer Plans" page and support FAQs on subscription pricing — [Garmin inReach Consumer Plans](https://www.garmin.com/en-US/p/837461/); [Garmin support: inReach subscription plan pricing FAQ](https://support.garmin.com/en-US/?faq=Z0mdgyBwqaAHP4QLy78UcA&productID=592606&tab=topics); [Garmin Blog: overview of inReach subscription plans](https://www.garmin.com/en-US/blog/outdoor/overview-inreach-subscription-plans/) — **pages located; pricing tables JS-gated, dollar amounts NOT captured (gap)**
- Third-party inReach cost roundups exist (secondhand, not fetched): [HikingGuy inReach subscription cost](https://hikingguy.com/garmin-inreach-subscription-cost/) — **flag: blog, verify against Garmin FAQ**

### Inferences
- Practical tiering for a prep site: FRS = $0 licensing, family-range line-of-sight comms; GMRS = $35/10yr, no exam, repeaters/family-wide use (best effort/reward ratio for households); Technician ham = $35/10yr + one 35-question exam, opens VHF/UHF + some HF and ARES/emcomm networks; inReach-class satellite = works when towers/backbones are down, but requires device purchase + recurring subscription.
- The "emergency message" carve-out in GMRS §95.1705(c)(3) and FRS one-way emergency messages (§95.531(b)) are citable hooks for disaster-comms guidance.

### Gaps
- Exact inReach plan tiers/prices (monthly/annual, Freedom vs.Contract, device prices) not captured — must be manually verified on Garmin's site; do not state numbers from memory.
- Ham exam pass rates, typical study time, and VEC exam fees (testing session fees are separate from the FCC $35) not captured.
- FRS/GMRS handheld price ranges (retail) not captured; no coverage-radius evidence (ERRP/power/antenna) sourced beyond "short-distance" regulatory language.
- ARES/RACES, winlink, and skywarn/ham-repeater infrastructure details not researched.

## Q4: Climate/location — "climate havens," First Street, NFIP, insurance retreat

### Takeaway
Credible research (First Street's flood modeling; Fifth National Climate Assessment coverage) supports "some places are safer than others" but rejects absolute havens — Great Lakes/Duluth/Ann Arbor/Burlington get named with caveats (wildfire smoke, inland flooding). Household financial prep should pair location choice with NFIP flood insurance (statutory caps: $250k building/$100k contents for single-family) and awareness of carrier retreat (State Farm CA non-renewals of ~30k home policies in 2024, citing catastrophe exposure/reinsurance).

### Cited Findings
- First Street First National Flood Risk Assessment (2020, full PDF fetched): nationally ~1.7× FEMA's 1-in-100 SFHA property count — 14.6 million properties at "substantial risk" (1% annual chance), of which 5.9 million are outside FEMA SFHA zones ("unaware of or underestimating the risk"); 10.3% of contiguous-US properties at substantial risk today, rising to 11.4% (≈16.2 million, +10.9%) by 2050; model built with Columbia, Fathom, George Mason, MIT, Rhodium Group, Rutgers, UC Berkeley, University of Bristol; free public tool Flood Factor — [First Street 2020 assessment PDF](https://assets.firststreet.org/media/first_street_foundation__first_national_flood_risk_assessment.pdf)
- First Street research library exists at [firststreet.org/research-library](https://firststreet.org/research-library) but as of Oct 2026 the whole firststreet.org domain is behind an MSCI terms-of-use interstitial (MSCI acquisition/gate observed during fetch); later reports (e.g., "15th Risk Assessment — Defining the Globe's Flood Risk") reachable only via CDN mirrors — [15th RA mirror (hubspot CDN, truncated in capture — search for full URL)](https://44226531.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/) — **flag access change; methodology page: [Flood model methodology](https://firststreet.org/methodology/flood)**
- Grist (Nov 20, 2023): named "climate havens" Duluth MN, Ann Arbor MI, Burlington VT; researchers point to Great Lakes/Michigan for freshwater, cooler summers, low hurricane/wildfire risk; but Fifth National Climate Assessment (Nov 2023) says "there's nowhere you can truly hide"; 2023 Canadian wildfire smoke hit the Midwest; July 2023 Vermont flash floods; ProPublica had earlier called Lamoille County VT "safest" before it flooded; Heatmap declared "It's time to put the idea of climate safe havens to rest"; FCA5 also documents relocation away from dangerous spots already happening (Southeast coasts, Midwest flood zones) — [Grist: Why 'climate havens' might be closer to home](https://grist.org/migration/climate-havens-national-climate-assessment-midwest-migration/)
- Climate-haven research leads (located via search, NOT fetched — flag): UMich Ross "Climate Havens as a Form of Transformative Climate Adaptation — Climate Havens Outlook 2026" PDF — [link](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/ajhoff/student_projects/Climate_Havens_Outlook_2026.pdf); New America "Great Lakes Receiving Network" (Mar/Apr 2026) — [link](https://www.newamerica.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Great-Lakes-Receiving-Network-PDF_March2026.pdf); GLISA (U-Michigan) "Future Climate Scenarios for Great Lakes Cities" — [link](https://glisa.umich.edu/resources-tools/future-climate-scenarios-for-great-lakes-cities/); Midwest Climate Resource Network Great Lakes maps — [link](https://midwestcrn.washu.edu/resource/great-lakes-regional-climate-change-maps/)
- NFIP statutory coverage limits (eCFR 44 CFR 61.6, current text via API): Regular Program max building coverage $250,000 single-family / $250,000 per unit condo building; contents (residential) $100,000; Emergency Program much lower ($35k/$10k SFD/residential contents) — [eCFR §61.6](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-44/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-61/section-61.6)
- NFIP buying mechanics (floodsmart.gov, live Oct 2026): policies for homeowners/renters/businesses; quotes via NFIP Policy Tool; "flood insurance policies do not automatically renew"; elevation certificates optional but can lower cost; discounts tool available — [floodsmart.gov Get Insured](https://www.floodsmart.gov/get-insured/buy-a-policy) (site nav/quote entry confirmed; premium averages not captured)
- Insurance-market retreat, primary source: State Farm General (press release, Mar 20, 2024): non-renew ~30,000 California homeowners/rental dwelling/residential community association/BOP policies (rolling from Jul 3, 2024) + withdraw commercial apartment with non-renewal of ~42,000 policies (from Aug 20, 2024); "just over 2% of State Farm General's policy count in California"; attributed to "inflation, catastrophe exposure, reinsurance costs, and … decades-old insurance regulations"; May 2023 halt on new CA applications unchanged — [State Farm newsroom: Update on California](https://newsroom.statefarm.com/update-on-california/)
- California regulatory context lead (not fetched): UCLA Anderson/Luskin "Eight Years After California's One-Year Non-Renewal Moratorium" (Mercury insurance/climate-change analysis, Winter 2025, dated 2026-01 on the PDF path) — [PDF](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/document/2026-01/uclaforecast_MercuryInsurance-Climate-Change-Analysis-Winter2025.pdf) — **flag: secondhand/academic forecast, verify before citing numbers**
- General explainer leads on climate-driven non-renewals (secondhand, not fetched): [Insurance.com on State Farm CA](https://www.insurance.com/home-insurance/state-farm-stops-selling-home-insurance-in-california)

### Inferences
- Defensible site framing: choose locations with lower *compound* risk (flood + heat + fire), but budget for insurance gaps — NFIP maxes out well below rebuild costs in high-cost states ($250k building cap), and private carriers have demonstrably retreated from CA (State Farm) even in non-coastal markets; check FAIR Plan reliance as a warning sign (mentioned in State Farm's release).
- First Street vs. FEMA discrepancies are the strongest citable reason a household outside a FEMA SFHA should still buy flood insurance (5.9M "unaware" properties; 1.7× factor).
