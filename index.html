Methodology

What is estimated. Each row is the chance of one kind of catastrophe happening by 2100, on the definition used by the Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament: more than 10% of humanity dying within about five years. The artificial intelligence row instead uses Joe Carlsmith's stricter outcome, humanity permanently losing control of its future, and the climate row also counts civilisation being permanently curtailed.

How each risk is built. Each risk is a chain of premises multiplied together. Every premise after the first is conditional on the one above it, so the product is the chance that the whole chain happens; the exact multiplication is shown under each category. Setting a premise to 0% switches off everything that depends on it. Artificial intelligence lowering the barrier to bioweapons and wartime attacks on infrastructure are folded into the relevant premises rather than given their own sliders.

Where the defaults come from. The artificial intelligence chain follows Carlsmith's "Is Power-Seeking AI an Existential Risk?" (2022), which estimates to 2070 rather than 2100. The defaults keep his figures for alignment difficulty, power-seeking and escalation, but use 85% for systems being built by 2100 and 60% for coordination failing, against his 65% and 80%. The other chains were assembled for this site; no published source presents them as chains. Their defaults were picked so each result lands inside the published range for that risk, for example nuclear catastrophe at 6% against 4–8% in the Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament. Figures under "What others say" marked "whole-chain risk" are estimates for the whole risk, not for that single premise, and are there for comparison.

Combined chance. Calculated as 1 − (1 − first risk) × (1 − second risk) × … across the six rows, which treats them as independent. They are not: shared premises push several risks up together, and one event can count twice, such as a nuclear war that also collapses the grid. Both effects mean the combined figure probably overstates the chance of at least one catastrophe.

Ranking prep. Each action has a weight for each risk: 2 if it helps a lot, 1 if it helps, 0.5 or 0.25 if it helps a little. The weights are this site's judgement, drawing on Ready.gov, Finland's 72-hours programme, the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, Boyd and Wilson (2023), Benjamin Todd, Zvi Mowshowitz and John-Clark Levin. "Chance of a catastrophe where this helps" is 1 − (1 − risk × weight ÷ 2) multiplied across the risks the action covers: roughly, the chance that a catastrophic scenario the action helps with actually happens. Actions are ranked by that figure alone. Cost is shown but not used. Ordinary emergencies such as storms and local outages are not counted, though that is where most of these items get used. The figure measures how often an action is relevant, not how much it improves the odds of survival.
